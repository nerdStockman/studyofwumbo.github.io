Apparent weight relative to the North Pole
How much does a mass appear to weigh at different places on Earth? Adjust the coordinates and mass below to explore the effect of Earth's rotation and equatorial bulge. The North Pole at sea level is the reference point. The model uses WGS84 normal gravity on the reference ellipsoid; it includes a WGS84 elevation correction, and adds a bundled EGM2008 gravity-disturbance grid for regional variations. This is a coarse model, not a local site survey. City elevations are approximate and can vary across a city.

Choose a location by adjusting the coordinates below, selecting a city preset, or clicking anywhere on the globe. You can also drag the globe to rotate it.

How it's calculated

WGS84 normal gravity at geodetic latitude φ on the reference ellipsoid is given by Somigliana's formula:

γ(φ,0) = γₑ × (1 + k sin²φ) / √(1 − e² sin²φ)

We apply the standard WGS84 normal-gravity height expansion, using ellipsoidal height h in meters:

γ(φ,h) = γ(φ,0) × [1 − (2h/a)(1 + f + m − 2f sin²φ) + 3h²/a²]

Constants: γₑ = 9.7803253359 m/s², k = 0.00193185265241, e² = 0.00669437999013, a = 6,378,137 m, f = 1/298.257223563, m ≈ 0.0034497865. The physical force is W = mγ newtons. Apparent weight is the gram-equivalent reading on a scale calibrated at the North Pole at h = 0 m: m × γ(φ,h)/γ(90°,0). The latitude effect is split into centrifugal reduction and the remaining oblate-shape contribution. The displayed local correction is a gravity disturbance, not a Bouguer anomaly.

City elevations are representative approximate heights above sea level, not surveyed WGS84 ellipsoidal heights. Differences between those two height systems, terrain, geology, and local gravity anomalies are not included unless a compatible grid is loaded. For precision experiments, enter a surveyed ellipsoidal height and use a local gravity model or measurement.

Reference: National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency — WGS84.
